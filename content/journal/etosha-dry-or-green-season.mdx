Etosha changes completely with the rain. The same park can feel like two different places, and the best time to go depends on what you want to see.

## The dry season (roughly June to October)

With little water anywhere else, animals come to the waterholes. Elephants, giraffe, zebra, springbok, oryx, lions and black rhino all need to drink, and they come to the same few places.

This is Etosha at its easiest. Park at a waterhole, switch off the engine and wait. In an hour the cast can change completely around you.

- **For:** concentrated wildlife, clear skies, cool mornings, the floodlit waterholes at their busiest.
- **Against:** dust, bleached landscapes, and more visitors, especially in July and August. October is very hot.

## The green season (roughly December to March)

When the rains come, pans fill everywhere and the animals spread out across fresh grass. Springbok and wildebeest give birth, and predators follow. In good years the great pan holds water, and flamingos may arrive to breed.

Hundreds of migrant bird species arrive. Storms build in the afternoons, and the light can be extraordinary.

- **For:** green landscapes, newborn animals, superb birds, dramatic skies, fewer people.
- **Against:** animals are harder to find, it's hot and humid, some roads get muddy, and malaria risk in the north is higher. *(Verify current health advice.)*

## In between

April, May and November are changeable months, and some of our favourites. In April the green fades to gold and the park is quiet. In November everything waits for the rain.

## After dark

Whatever the season, stay at least two nights inside the park or right at its edge. The floodlit waterholes at the rest camps are where some of Etosha's best sightings happen, often black rhino, often after dinner. In the dry season, bring a warm layer.

## So which?

If it's your first visit and wildlife is the priority, go in the dry season. If you've been before, love birds, or want drama and space, try the green season.
